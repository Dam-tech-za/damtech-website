# El Niño 2026/27: Impacts on South African Farmers, Water Security and Agriculture

**Research report** · Prepared 5 November 2026 · Damtech

> **Scope.** This report reviews what the 2026/27 El Niño is likely to mean for South African agriculture — field crops, livestock, horticulture and irrigation — and for water security at farm, municipal and national level. It draws on official forecasts (South African Weather Service, NOAA, SADC), government advisories, industry analysis and the historical record of past El Niño seasons. Seasonal forecasts describe probabilities, not certainties; figures quoted here are the latest available at the time of writing and will change as the season unfolds.

## 1. Executive summary

- **A very strong El Niño is under way.** NOAA declared El Niño conditions in June 2026 and put the odds of a "very strong" event at about 63%. On 1 September the South African Weather Service (SAWS) said sea-surface temperature anomalies in the east-central Pacific had already crossed the very-strong threshold and that the event could become the strongest in living memory, peaking between roughly October 2026 and January 2027.
- **The most likely outcome is a hotter, drier summer** over most of South Africa. SAWS and the SADC regional forecast both favour below-normal rainfall for October–December 2026 and November 2026–January 2027, with above-normal temperatures and more frequent heatwaves. SAWS expects 2027 to be the warmest year on record globally.
- **Strength does not equal impact.** Of the three very strong El Niños since 1979, 1982/83 and 2015/16 brought widespread drought, but 1997/98 brought slightly above-normal rain to parts of the summer rainfall region. Local outcomes will depend on other drivers such as the Indian Ocean Dipole.
- **South Africa enters the season with unusually strong buffers:** national dam storage of roughly 95% (versus about 55% in August 2016), good soil moisture and veld after a long, wet 2025/26 La Niña season, and a record 2025/26 maize crop (now estimated at 17.7 million tonnes against consumption of about 12 million tonnes).
- **But the buffers are uneven.** Full dams protect irrigated farming (about 20% of field crops, plus nearly all fruit and vegetables), not the ~80% of field crops that are rain-fed. The Western Cape enters summer after one of its driest winters in decades, with its main supply system near 79% and a wheat crop at an eight-year low. Municipal water systems in Gauteng and eThekwini remain vulnerable to outages because of leaks and pumping constraints, regardless of dam levels.
- **Farmers are already adjusting.** Intended maize plantings for 2026/27 are down about 12% to 2.39 million hectares (an eight-year low), while sunflower intentions are up by roughly 45% as growers shift to a shorter-season, more drought-tolerant crop.
- **Food price effects will be delayed, not avoided.** Food inflation is currently very low (0.6% in July 2026, the lowest since 2010), and existing grain stocks should cover early 2027. The real test comes from mid-2027, when the 2026/27 crop reaches market. In the 2015/16 drought, maize output fell to about 8.9 million tonnes, South Africa had to import, and food inflation averaged 10.8% in 2016.
- **Livestock is the most exposed sector in a drought year**, and this season it is layered on top of an ongoing foot-and-mouth disease outbreak that was declared a national disaster in early 2026. In 2015/16 around 186,000 animals died and nearly 247,000 farmers were affected.
- **The key message from SAWS and disaster managers is to prepare early and manage conservatively**: protect soil moisture, avoid marginal lands, align herd sizes to grazing, secure feed and water, fix leaks, and plan for heat and fire.

## 2. The climate picture: how strong is this El Niño?

### 2.1 Global forecasts

| Date | Agency | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 12 March 2026 | NOAA Climate Prediction Center (CPC) | La Niña Advisory plus El Niño Watch issued simultaneously |
| 14 May 2026 | NOAA CPC | 82% chance of El Niño forming May–July; 96% chance of it persisting into Dec 2026–Feb 2027 |
| 11 June 2026 | NOAA CPC | El Niño Advisory issued — conditions present; ~63% chance of a "very strong" event |
| 28 August 2026 | SADC (SARCOF-33) | Below-normal rainfall favoured over most of South Africa for Oct–Dec 2026; drier conditions over parts of the country for Jan–Mar 2027 |
| 1 September 2026 | SAWS | Weekly Pacific SST anomalies already above the very-strong threshold; event may become strongest in living memory |

NOAA linked its high confidence to unusually high oceanic heat content and expanding westerly wind anomalies across the equatorial Pacific. Climate scientists quoted in the South African press have described the event as possibly the most intense on record, and it is not expected to fade before around February–March 2027 — close to the end of the summer cropping season.

### 2.2 The South African outlook

SAWS's seasonal outlook favours **below-normal rainfall over most of the country for October–December 2026**, with **November 2026–January 2027 looking very similar**. Above-normal temperatures are considered very likely. SAWS warned that, if the expected dry conditions materialise, heatwaves will tend to occur more often, and it expects the combination of this El Niño and global warming to make 2027 the warmest year yet recorded.

SAWS has singled out the **far north-east of the country (including the Kruger National Park area)** as likely to be hardest hit. For KwaZulu-Natal, SAWS expects El Niño effects from October 2026 to March 2027.

Early-season conditions are consistent with the forecast: after misleading September rains, hot and dry weather dominated much of the grain belt in October and slowed early planting.

### 2.3 An important caveat

SAWS itself has cautioned that the magnitude of the El Niño should not be read as a direct measure of how severe or widespread the impacts will be over southern Africa, and that not every El Niño brings drought. Its senior manager for disaster risk reduction has stressed that El Niño is not in itself a disaster — the disaster comes from unpreparedness. SAWS's practical advice is that **a conservative management strategy is advisable in El Niño summers.**

## 3. Lessons from past El Niño seasons

SAWS has identified **14 El Niño events since 1979**: five weak, five moderate, one strong and three very strong (1982/83, 1997/98 and 2015/16).

| Season | Strength | What happened in South Africa |
|---|---|---|
| 1982/83 | Very strong | Widespread drought |
| 1997/98 | Very strong | Slightly **above-normal** rain over parts of the summer rainfall region — a reminder that strong events don't guarantee drought |
| 2014/15–2015/16 | Very strong | Severe, multi-year drought. Maize output averaged about 8.9 Mt across the two seasons; South Africa imported maize; food inflation averaged 10.8% in 2016. Losses also hit wheat, soybeans, sunflower and sugarcane. National dam storage was about 55% by August 2016 |
| 2018/19 | — | Severe drought; maize averaged about 11.8 Mt |
| 2023/24 | Strong | Mid-summer dry spell and heat in February 2024; maize fell about 22% to 12.9 Mt |

### What 2015/16 cost the farming sector

- About **246,600 small, medium and large-scale farmers** were affected, around 153,000 of them in KwaZulu-Natal.
- Roughly **186,000 livestock died**, with the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga hardest hit.
- Government announced a **R1 billion relief package**; by March 2016 only about 42,000 farmers had been assisted.
- Of 89 emergency boreholes drilled, 29 were dry — a sign that groundwater is not an automatic fallback in a deep drought.
- Researchers at the time warned that loss of breeding stock would cause a **shortage of livestock products for two to four years** afterwards.

### Yield lesson

Agricultural economist Wandile Sihlobo notes that in the severe 2013/14–2015/16 period maize yields fell to about 3.5 t/ha, compared with more than 5 t/ha in La Niña years, while the two milder El Niño seasons since then kept yields above 4 t/ha. The difference between a "mild" and a "severe" El Niño is therefore worth several million tonnes of maize.

## 4. Starting position: South Africa's buffers going into 2026/27

South Africa is better placed than in 2015 on most national indicators.

| Buffer | Position in 2026 | Comparison |
|---|---|---|
| National dam storage | ~95.5% in August; ~95.6% on 2 November 2026 | ~55% in August 2016 |
| Integrated Vaal River System | ~98–100% | Supports roughly 46% of the economy |
| Soil moisture & water tables | Rains continued until about May 2026, well past the usual March end | Much drier start in 2015 |
| Veld/grazing | Fair condition after extended rains | Poor in 2015 |
| 2025/26 maize crop | **17.7 Mt** (CEC 9th estimate, record) | Consumption ~12 Mt |
| 2025/26 total summer grains & oilseeds | ~21.5 Mt; record soybeans (3.04 Mt) | — |
| Opening maize stocks | ~2.4 Mt | — |
| Food inflation | 0.6% (July 2026), lowest since 2010 | Averaged 10.8% in 2016 |

### Where the buffers are thin

1. **Rain-fed agriculture.** Dams protect irrigators. The National Agricultural Marketing Council (NAMC) has pointed out that high dam levels may mean little for rain-dependent producers, who farm the large majority of grain and grazing land.
2. **The Western Cape.** Winter rainfall areas had a poor season. Cape Town International Airport recorded only 14.4 mm in July 2026, its lowest July total since 1958. By 7 September the Western Cape Water Supply System was at 79.5% (84.7% a year earlier), with Voëlvlei at 59% and Steenbras Lower at 47% — both full a year before. Cape Town has entered a "drought-caution" phase.
3. **Farm finances.** Farmers face high fertiliser, diesel and financing costs, while grain prices have fallen (white maize ~R3,700/t and yellow ~R3,800/t at end-October, down from over R4,000/t a month earlier). Cash-flow and insurance access are particular constraints for smallholder and emerging farmers.
4. **Animal health.** The foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) outbreak, declared a national disaster in early 2026, has already disrupted trade, slaughter and herd movements.
5. **Municipal water infrastructure.** Full dams cannot overcome leaking pipes and inadequate pumping (see Section 8).

Southern African Agri Initiative chair Dr Theo de Jager has called 2026 a "perfect storm" year for farmers, combining floods, FMD and input-cost pressure before El Niño even arrives.

## 5. Impacts on field crops

### 5.1 Summer grains and oilseeds

**Planting decisions are already shifting.** The Crop Estimates Committee's first intentions-to-plant figures (released 27 October 2026) show:

| Crop | 2026/27 intention | Change |
|---|---|---|
| All summer crops (maize, sunflower, soybeans, groundnuts, sorghum, dry beans) | ~4.5 million ha | −2% (close to 5-year average) |
| Maize (total) | 2.387 million ha | −12.4% — lowest in eight years |
| White maize | — | down more than 18% |
| Sunflower | — | up almost 45% |

Drivers of the shift:

- **Season length.** Grain SA expects a shorter growing season and says farmers want crops that mature faster. Sunflower has a shorter season, lower input costs and better drought tolerance.
- **Early planting to use stored moisture.** In western production areas some sunflower was planted about two months earlier than usual, in August, to exploit residual soil moisture.
- **Economics.** Lower maize prices, large white-maize stocks and rising fertiliser and fuel costs have all discouraged maize planting.

**Yield risk.** Roughly 80% of field crops are rain-fed. The critical periods for maize are establishment (November–December) and especially **flowering and grain-fill (late January–February)**. The 2023/24 season showed that even a mild mid-summer dry spell during this window can cut national maize output by over 20%. A 2015/16-type outcome would push production back below domestic consumption.

**Timing of the market impact.** Crops planted from October 2026 are harvested from about April–May 2027. South Africa will rely mainly on the 2025/26 crop for the first quarter and part of the second quarter of 2027; the El Niño effect on grain supplies will be felt from mid-2027.

### 5.2 Winter cereals

The Western Cape grows about two-thirds of South Africa's wheat. Its dry winter has left the crop in poor condition:

- 2026/27 wheat area: about 479,500 ha — the lowest in 13 years (−7%).
- First production estimate: about 1.8 Mt — down 8% and the lowest in eight years.
- South Africa already imports around 1.85 Mt of wheat a year; a smaller crop raises import needs and exposure to global prices and the exchange rate.

A hot, dry spring and summer will also pressure next year's winter crop if Western Cape dams and soil moisture are not replenished before the 2027 planting season.

### 5.3 Pests and disease

The Department of Agriculture warns that many pests and diseases thrive in hot, dry conditions and advises regular scouting. Heat stress also makes crops more vulnerable.

## 6. Impacts on livestock

Livestock farming is usually the most severely affected sector in an El Niño drought because grazing, drinking water and animal condition deteriorate together, and recovery of breeding herds takes years.

### 6.1 Main risks

- **Grazing loss and overgrazing** as veld growth slows under heat and low rainfall; risk of livestock deaths where stocking rates are too high.
- **Heat stress**, reducing feed intake, fertility, conception rates, milk yield and weaning weights.
- **Water shortages** at boreholes, earth dams and drinking points.
- **Higher feed costs** if grain and roughage supplies tighten.
- **Disease and parasite pressure**, compounded by the existing FMD outbreak and movement restrictions.
- **Forced selling** into a weak market, followed by herd-rebuilding costs and a multi-year supply gap.

### 6.2 The FMD overlay

The FMD outbreak — centred on KwaZulu-Natal, where over 17,000 farms had been affected by February 2026 — led to a national disaster declaration and a mass vaccination drive targeting around 12 million cattle. More than 297,000 cattle had been affected and over 120,000 culled by late February. Export markets such as China closed to South African meat. This has two consequences in an El Niño year:

1. Movement and slaughter restrictions limit farmers' ability to destock quickly when grazing runs out.
2. With export channels closed, more meat stays in the local market, which has helped keep meat inflation low in 2026 — but a drought-driven sell-off followed by herd rebuilding could reverse that in 2027–28.

### 6.3 Breeding-season management (2026/27)

Advice from the Red Meat Industry Services and university specialists for this season includes:

- Plan breeding around the weather outlook; align mating with peak natural grazing where practical.
- Aim for a body condition score of about **3.5** at the start of mating; breed heifers at **60–65%** of mature weight.
- Complete pre-breeding vaccinations about **30 days** before mating; give trace minerals **4–6 weeks** before.
- Bring new bulls in about **two months** before breeding and test bull fertility (breeding-soundness evaluation).
- Control ticks, mites and internal parasites through an area-specific programme.

### 6.4 Herd and grazing management

The Department of Agriculture advises:

- Keep herd sizes in line with veld carrying capacity; sell older or unproductive animals early.
- Practise rotational grazing.
- **Increase the number of water and feed points**.
- Keep feed reserves for the breeding herd and supplement where necessary.
- Maintain veterinary inspections, disease surveillance and vaccinations.

## 7. Horticulture, irrigation and sugar

### 7.1 Irrigated horticulture

Virtually all South African fruit and vegetable production is irrigated, and irrigation dams in most summer-rainfall areas are full. This makes horticulture relatively well protected in 2026/27 **outside the Western Cape**. Key risks are:

- **Heat damage** (sunburn, poor fruit set, quality and size losses) during heatwaves.
- **Higher evaporation and water demand**, which draws dams down faster than usual.
- **Allocation cuts** in the Western Cape if the November storage review or later reviews trigger restrictions. The Department of Water and Sanitation (DWS) regulates agricultural allocations and has said domestic supply and strategic users get priority when supplies tighten.
- **Electricity and pumping costs** rise with heavier irrigation.

### 7.2 Water-use efficiency on irrigated farms

The Department of Agriculture's advisory recommends drip and other efficient systems, irrigating in the early morning or evening, water harvesting, and strict compliance with local restrictions.

### 7.3 Sugarcane

Sugarcane is largely rain-fed in KwaZulu-Natal and suffered substantial losses in 2015/16. With KZN expected to experience El Niño effects from October 2026 to March 2027 and veld-fire risk elevated, cane growers face yield and fire-loss risk this season.

## 8. Water security

### 8.1 Bulk storage: strong nationally

National dam storage was about **95.6% on 2 November 2026**. Provincial averages ranged from about 99.7% (Free State) and 99.6% (North West) to 87.6% (KwaZulu-Natal) and 78.5% (Western Cape). The Integrated Vaal River System was around 98–100%, and the uMngeni system serving Durban about 90%.

This is a very different starting point from 2015/16, and it means **large-scale bulk-supply failure in summer-rainfall regions is unlikely this season** even if rainfall is poor. A prolonged El Niño that extends into a second dry year would be a different matter.

### 8.2 The real vulnerability: distribution, not storage

DWS and analysts agree that the main water-security risk this summer lies in municipal systems:

| Indicator | Figure |
|---|---|
| National non-revenue water (No Drop programme) | 47.3% — about 2.1 billion kL a year, worth ~R26 billion |
| Gauteng | ~27% physical losses; ~47% non-revenue water |
| eThekwini | ~40% physical losses; ~56% non-revenue water |
| Average consumption | ~250 L per person per day (international average ~173 L) |

Hotter, drier weather increases demand and can prolong outages between October 2026 and March 2027. Gauteng municipalities supplied by Rand Water and eThekwini and surrounding areas supplied by uMngeni-uThukela Water are seen as most vulnerable. DWS has noted that recent outages were not caused by drought but by leaks, ageing pumps and insufficient storage and pumping capacity. It estimates that **cutting municipal demand by about 10%** in these regions would greatly reduce the risk of summer outages.

Municipalities have historically been slow to impose and enforce water restrictions. DWS regulates industrial and agricultural users directly; if supplies tighten, domestic drinking water and strategic users such as Eskom receive priority.

### 8.3 Western Cape

The Western Cape is the main exception to the "full dams" picture. After a very dry winter, the province enters the hot, dry season with its major supply system around 79–80% and several key dams far below last year. DWS indicated it would review storage in November before deciding on restrictions. Agricultural allocations from the Berg and Breede systems are at risk if a dry summer is followed by another poor winter.

### 8.4 Rural and farm water

- Many rural communities and farms depend on **boreholes and small earth dams**, which respond quickly to dry spells and high evaporation. The 2015/16 experience — almost a third of emergency boreholes coming up dry — shows the limits of groundwater as a crisis fallback.
- Livestock water points are a critical weakness during heatwaves, when animal water demand rises sharply.
- On-farm water storage (tanks, reservoirs, lined dams), rainwater harvesting and leak repair are the most direct, controllable investments farmers can make before the peak of the season.

### 8.5 Water quality and health

Intermittent supply leads households to store water in unsuitable containers, raising the risk of diarrhoeal disease and cholera. Heavy downpours on dry ground or blocked drainage can also contaminate supplies — El Niño summers can still bring damaging storms and flash floods.

## 9. Food prices and the wider economy

### 9.1 Near term (late 2026 – Q1 2027): subdued

- Consumer food inflation fell to **0.6% in July 2026**, the lowest since 2010, helped by record harvests, grain-product deflation and higher meat supplies (due to FMD export bans).
- Large grain stocks will carry the market into early 2027.
- The bigger near-term risk is **fuel**: about 80% of staple foods move by road, and diesel prices have risen sharply in 2026 because of tensions in the Middle East.

### 9.2 Medium term (mid-2027 onwards): the El Niño test

- Agbiz regards the El Niño drought as an **upside risk to food inflation in 2027**, with the timing driven by when the 2026/27 harvest reaches the market.
- Agbiz also argues that forecasts of a 2016-style inflation spike may underestimate today's buffers (soil moisture, dam levels, grain stocks), so this drought should not automatically be assumed to replicate 2015/16.
- Food has a weight of about **16.8%** in the CPI basket; a grain-driven food price shock would also feed into animal feed, meat, poultry, dairy and egg prices.
- Grain prices are expected to rise during the season as southern African demand picks up. Regional neighbours, where much farming is subsistence and rain-fed, could face severe food shortages and turn to South Africa for maize — at a time when humanitarian aid budgets have been cut.

### 9.3 Farm incomes

A smaller crop with higher prices helps some producers, but those with crop failure face losses on high input costs. Livestock producers forced to sell in a drought face low prices, then high replacement costs. Smallholders, emerging farmers and those without insurance carry the greatest risk.

### 9.4 Other sectors

- **Mining and industry**: water and power supply risks, plus a potential swing from drought to flood events.
- **Insurance**: higher fire, crop and property claims.
- **Electricity**: higher cooling and irrigation pumping demand during heatwaves.

## 10. Fire, heat and human health

### 10.1 Veld fires

The heavy rains of early 2026 produced abundant grass growth. As it dries under heat and low rainfall, this becomes a large fuel load. KwaZulu-Natal has identified six high-fire-risk municipalities (Nkandla, uMfolozi, Nongoma, uMshwathi, eDumbe and oKhahlamba) and distributed firefighting equipment. Farmers should:

- Prepare and maintain firebreaks; join or support Fire Protection Associations.
- Check firefighting equipment and train workers.
- Protect fodder banks, grazing camps and infrastructure.

### 10.2 Heat and health

Extreme heat increases the risk of dehydration, heat exhaustion and heatstroke, and worsens heart, lung and kidney disease. Farm workers and other outdoor labourers are among those most at risk, along with older people, infants and pregnant women. Employers are advised to provide water, shade, rest breaks and trained supervisors, and to review heat-exposure policies.

Food price shocks can also worsen nutrition — particularly protein and micronutrient intake — among poor households, and income pressure can disrupt chronic medication and clinic visits.

## 12. Policy and institutional response

### 12.1 What government is doing

- **SAWS**: monthly seasonal outlooks and daily extreme-weather warnings.
- **Department of Agriculture**: El Niño advisory issued 2 September 2026; monthly National Agro-meteorological Committee advisories; awareness work with provinces.
- **National Disaster Management Centre**: July 2026 advisory calling for stronger drought preparedness, contingency plans and public awareness. Disaster classification under section 23 of the Disaster Management Act unlocks recovery funding once a disaster exceeds local coping capacity.
- **DWS**: consultations with water boards, catchment agencies, municipalities, the agriculture sector and the Agricultural Research Council on supply and allocation measures. DWS has said that additional El Niño funding is not considered necessary at this stage, with the government's disaster contingency reserve available if needed.
- **Support instruments**: drought-tolerant cultivars on the National Variety List; the Comprehensive Agricultural Support Programme (infrastructure, inputs, irrigation, mechanisation); and the Blended Finance Scheme, which requires funded farmers to insure (premium about 6% of the approved loan).
- **Provinces**: e.g. KwaZulu-Natal's El Niño awareness campaign rolled out to all municipalities, with fire equipment for high-risk areas.

### 12.2 Gaps

- No dedicated, pre-funded drought-relief allocation has been announced; relief is reactive and dependent on disaster declarations, which in 2015/16 reached only a fraction of affected farmers.
- Municipal leak repair and demand management — the most effective water-security measures — depend on municipalities that have historically been slow to act.
- Insurance coverage among smallholders and emerging farmers remains limited.

## 13. Scenarios for the 2026/27 season

| | **A. Mild / 1997/98-type** | **B. Moderate / 2023/24-type** | **C. Severe / 2015/16-type** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rainfall | Near-normal in parts of the summer region; dry spells manageable | Reasonable start; damaging mid-summer dry spell and heat (Jan–Feb) | Widespread, persistent below-normal rain and repeated heatwaves |
| Maize output (indicative) | ~13–15 Mt | ~11–13 Mt (a 2023/24-type ~20% fall from trend) | <10 Mt; imports needed |
| Livestock | Localised stress | Condition losses, some forced selling | Large-scale deaths and destocking; multi-year supply gap |
| Water | Bulk supply secure; municipal outages from leaks | Bulk secure; Western Cape restrictions likely if next winter is dry | Pressure on bulk systems by late 2027, especially the Western Cape |
| Food inflation 2027 | Little change | Moderate rise from mid-2027 | Sharp rise; 2016 averaged 10.8% |

*Maize ranges are illustrative, based on the reduced planted area and historical El Niño yields cited in this report; they are not official forecasts.*

Given current forecasts, **Scenario B is a reasonable planning base, with Scenario C as the stress test**. Strong buffers make the extreme 2015/16 outcome less likely in its national impact, but not for individual rain-fed farms or regions such as the far north-east.

## 14. Recommendations

### 14.1 Crop farmers

1. **Protect soil moisture**: minimum or no-till, residue retention, conservation agriculture implements.
2. **Don't expand into marginal fields**; consider reducing high-input plantings on lower-potential soils.
3. **Choose heat- and drought-tolerant cultivars** from the National Variety List; consider shorter-season crops (sunflower, sorghum, early-maturing maize).
4. **Stay within planting windows** and stagger plantings where possible to avoid all fields flowering in the same dry spell.
5. **Review input budgets and fertiliser rates** against realistic yield targets.
6. **Insure** where possible and review cash-flow and credit arrangements early.
7. **Scout regularly** for pests and diseases.

### 14.2 Livestock farmers

1. **Match stocking rates to carrying capacity now**; sell older and unproductive animals early while condition and prices are reasonable.
2. **Secure fodder and feed reserves** for the breeding herd before prices rise.
3. **Add and protect water points**; check boreholes, pumps, troughs and pipelines; store water.
4. **Rotate grazing** and rest camps; protect a drought reserve.
5. **Keep vaccinations and parasite control current**, and comply with FMD controls.
6. **Plan breeding around the outlook** (condition scores, heifer weights, bull fertility testing).

### 14.3 Irrigation farmers

1. Plan for higher evaporation and demand; schedule irrigation using soil-moisture or ET data.
2. Use efficient systems (drip, micro) and irrigate in the cool of the day.
3. Fix leaks in canals, pipelines and storage; line or cover dams where feasible.
4. Western Cape growers should prepare for possible allocation cuts and prioritise high-value blocks.

### 14.4 Municipalities and water boards

1. **Fix major leaks and reduce non-revenue water** — the single biggest lever for summer supply security.
2. Introduce **demand-management and restrictions early**, with consistent enforcement; target a ~10% demand cut in Gauteng and eThekwini.
3. Secure **backup supply for clinics, schools and hospitals**; monitor water quality.
4. Maintain pumps and reservoirs and pre-position emergency tankers and repair crews.

### 14.5 Government and agribusiness

1. Pre-position drought-relief mechanisms (feed, water, finance) rather than waiting for disaster declarations.
2. Make forecasts **usable at farm level** through extension services, especially for smallholders.
3. Expand insurance and blended finance for emerging farmers.
4. Protect school-feeding and nutrition programmes ahead of a possible food price rise.
5. Monitor regional food security; plan grain logistics for exports to neighbours and possible imports later in 2027.

## 15. What to monitor, and when

| When | Indicator | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly | SAWS seasonal outlook; NOAA/BoM ENSO updates; Indian Ocean Dipole | Whether the dry signal strengthens, weakens or shifts geographically |
| Weekly | DWS reservoir reports (Vaal, uMngeni, Western Cape systems); local dams and boreholes | Supply and restriction risk |
| Nov–Dec 2026 | Rain onset, dry-spell length, temperature, planting progress | Crop establishment |
| Late Jan 2027 | CEC preliminary area planted | Actual vs intended maize/sunflower area |
