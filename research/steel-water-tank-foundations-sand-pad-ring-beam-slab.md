# Foundations for Lined Corrugated Steel Water Tanks: Sand Pad, Ring Beam or Slab

**Research report** · Prepared 12 November 2026 · Damtech

> **Scope.** This report compares the base options for ground-level, lined, corrugated steel water tanks of the kind Damtech supplies: Z275 galvanised or AZ150 Aluzinc shells from 3 m to about 17 m in diameter and 1.55 m to 3.05 m high, with a 550 gsm PVC liner on a bidem floor sheet. It covers compacted granular pads, concrete ring beams with compacted fill, full reinforced slabs, and engineered foundations for problem ground. For each it sets out how the base works with the tank, its advantages and disadvantages, and where it fits. The report also covers South African ground hazards (dolomite, expansive clay, compressible and collapsible soils) and the coating question at the steel-to-concrete joint. It is technical decision support, not a foundation design. Every site needs its own assessment, and no dimension here is for construction. Evidence levels stay visible because many of the published numbers come from vendors rather than standards.

## 1. Executive summary

**For a lined corrugated tank, the base exists to keep the liner flat and supported, the wall base level and dry, and the edge from washing out. Bearing capacity is rarely the problem; uneven settlement, erosion and water at the wall base are.** A full tank of Damtech's standard heights puts only about 15, 23 and 30 kPa on its floor (1.55 m, 2.3 m and 3.05 m of water) [Der]. Most competent, compacted ground carries that. What damages a lined tank is a soft edge, a dished or tilted floor, a scoured perimeter, or dirt and water held against the bottom of the wall.

**The three mainstream options suit different jobs.**

- **Compacted granular pad (sand or crusher dust on geotextile)** is the cheapest and fastest, drains freely and suits small and medium tanks on stable, level ground. Its weaknesses are the edge: it erodes, spreads and settles at the perimeter, and it gives nothing to anchor a tank to. It is Damtech's current default and stays right for most kit sizes on good ground.
- **Concrete ring beam with compacted fill inside** keeps the granular floor (so the liner still sits on a soft, free-draining bed) but adds a rigid, level perimeter that carries the wall, stops edge spread and washout, and gives a fixing point for anchors. API 650 guidance lists these benefits for oil tanks, and vendors recommend ringwalls from about 6 m (20 ft) diameter. It is the strongest general-purpose upgrade for larger tanks, roofed tanks on windy sites, sloping sites and erodible sandy ground.
- **Full reinforced slab** gives the most uniform, level and durable surface and the best control of leaks, because water that passes the liner can be made visible at the edge instead of soaking into the ground. It costs most, needs curing time, must be finished smooth and covered with an underlay to protect the liner, and does not by itself stop edge settlement on weak soil. It earns its place on problem ground, for potable or fire-water duty, and where a leak must never reach the subsoil.

**South African ground changes the answer more than tank size does.** About a quarter of Gauteng is dolomitic, and leaking water-bearing services trigger most sinkholes. A water tank is a water-bearing structure, so on dolomite land (Centurion and other parts of Tshwane among them) no base should be chosen before the site's dolomite designation is known and a competent person has been consulted. On expansive clays, which cover large parts of Gauteng and the Free State, a leak causes local, differential heave. On both, a slab with a controlled leak path, or an engineered raft, replaces the sand pad.

**At the wall base, galvanised and Aluzinc behave differently.** Zinc reacts with fresh concrete during curing but is passivated once the concrete has set, so a galvanised shell can bear on cured concrete. Aluzinc should not touch concrete at all, cured or not, if it can get wet. On any ring beam or slab, a bituminous separator strip under the wall and a sealed, draining joint should be standard, and soil, gravel and vegetation must be kept off the bottom of the wall for every base type.

**Engineering liability frames the website use.** No public source found here, including the Pioneer catalogues, gives ring beam dimensions for small liner tanks. Damtech should publish base guidance as typical options with an engineer's caveat, and any typical ring beam detail should come from Damtech's own Pr.Eng sign-off, not from another supplier's drawings.

## 2. How a lined corrugated tank loads its base

This section explains why the API 650 oil-tank literature, which dominates the sources, transfers only partly to Damtech's tanks.

**The liner, not the steel floor, holds the water.** In an API 650 welded tank or an AWWA D103 bolted tank, the shell and a steel floor plate form the vessel, and the shell carries the roof and its own weight down onto a narrow annular strip. A corrugated liner tank has no steel floor. The PVC liner lies on a protective floor sheet over the base and turns up the inside of the wall. The water's weight goes straight through the liner into the base, spread over the whole floor. The wall carries the hoop tension from the water pressure and its own weight plus any roof, which is a much lighter line load than a welded oil-tank shell [Inference from the tank construction].

**Floor pressure is modest and set by water depth alone** [Der]:

| Water depth (Damtech ring count) | Floor pressure, full |
|---|---|
| 1.55 m (two rings) | about 15 kPa |
| 2.3 m (three rings) | about 23 kPa |
| 3.05 m (four rings) | about 30 kPa |

The diameter does not change the pressure, but it changes the total load and the area over which ground conditions vary. A 17 m tank covers about 227 m² and holds about 350 t of water at 1.55 m; a 3 m tank covers about 7 m².

**Three consequences follow.**

1. **The liner tolerates no point loads or steps.** Stones, roots, a ridge at a ring beam joint or a cracked slab edge can puncture or overstress it. Every base option must end in a smooth, fine, uniform bed under the floor sheet.
2. **Differential settlement matters more than total settlement.** A uniform settlement of a few millimetres is harmless. A dish in the middle, a soft edge or a tilt stretches the liner, puts the wall out of round and leaves a lower sector that holds dirt and water at the wall base.
3. **An empty tank is light.** A corrugated shell, especially with a roof, is a large, light structure. When empty or nearly empty it can be lifted, slid or racked by wind. A granular pad gives no restraint; a ring beam or slab can take anchors. No source read gives wind uplift figures for these tanks (see section 10).

## 3. Option A: compacted granular pad

**What it is.** A level platform of compacted sand, crusher dust or road-base gravel, laid on stripped and compacted ground, usually with geotextile under or over the fill, extending beyond the tank. The liner floor sheet sits directly on the finished surface.

**How it complements the tank.** The fine, slightly yielding surface is kind to the liner and spreads any small unevenness. It drains, so condensation or minor seepage under the floor does not pond. It can be built with light plant in a day or two, and the tank can be moved later.

**Advantages**

- Lowest cost and fastest to build; no curing time [Prod: Tank Factory, Coerco].
- Free-draining; moisture is carried away from the underside [Sec: OEP study guide].
- Soft bed for the liner; easy to dress level by hand.
- Relocatable and easy to repair or re-level.
- Uses local materials and skills available on most farms.

**Disadvantages**

- **Edge failure is the main risk.** Under the wall, granular fill spreads sideways, slumps and settles, and rain or overflow washes it out, leaving voids under the bottom ring [Sec: OEP; EPCLand; Tarsco].
- No anchorage against wind on an empty or roofed tank.
- Erosion needs a confining edge or a protected shoulder. Bushman makes a 75 mm × 400 mm aggregate band round the tank a warranty condition, to stop roof run-off scouring the pad [Prod].
- Performance depends wholly on the subgrade. On soft clay a granular pad under a large tank settled more than 150 mm in three years in one reported case [Sec: EPCLand, anecdotal].
- On dolomite or expansive clay, any liner leak passes straight into the ground (section 8).
- The edge must be maintained for the tank's life; a sand ring "must be maintained" (Bushman general guidance) [Prod].

**Typical published details** (vendor figures, not standards; they conflict):

| Item | Range found | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Fill depth | 70–75 mm (poly tanks) to 150 mm minimum (steel liner tanks) | Coerco; Bushman Aqualine; Pioneer drawing (about 150 mm, 6 in) |
| Extension beyond tank | 300 mm total width to more than 2 m wider than the diameter | Tank Factory; Bushman Aqualine |
| Fill grading | Crusher dust under 5 mm, with geotextile over it to protect the liner | Bushman Aqualine |
| Level | "100% level"; "level grade assumed" | Bushman; Pioneer drawing |
| Shoulder slope (large tanks) | At least 1:20 falling away, shoulder 0.9–1.5 m wide | EPCLand (unsourced) |
| Erosion protection | 75 mm aggregate band 400 mm wide; or about 75 mm of 25–75 mm rock | Bushman; Pioneer drawing |

**Where it fits.** Small and medium tanks (Damtech's 3 m to about 6 m kits) on firm, level, non-problem ground, unroofed or in sheltered positions, where the owner will keep the edge and drainage maintained.

**Improving it cheaply.** A confined granular pad (the fill retained by a ring of blocks, a steel ring or a timber frame) removes much of the edge-spread and washout risk at low cost. Coerco and Bushman both describe framed or ringed pads [Prod]. It still provides no anchorage.

## 4. Option B: concrete ring beam with compacted fill

**What it is.** A reinforced concrete ring (ringwall) under the tank wall, with compacted granular fill inside it finished level with, or just below, the top of the ring, and the liner floor sheet over the fill. The wall stands on the ring.

**How it complements the tank.** It splits the two jobs. The ring gives the wall a rigid, level, durable seat and holds the fill in; the fill gives the liner the same soft, draining bed as a pad. That is why bolted-tank and corrugated-tank suppliers specify a gravel base "contained by a concrete ringwall" [Prod: Tank Connection] and use ring beams for commercial-size corrugated tanks [Prod: GEI Works].

**Advantages** (API 650's list, as reported by Padros, plus other sources)

- Distributes the wall load evenly and provides a flat, level surface for erecting the shell [Sec: Padros, citing API 650].
- Gives a level datum for building the tank and checking it later [Sec: Padros; OEP].
- Retains the fill and prevents its loss by erosion [Sec: Padros, citing API 650].
- Reduces moisture under the tank and keeps the wall base off wet soil [Sec: Padros; OEP].
- Resists edge settlement and sideways spread of the fill [Sec: OEP; Tarsco, vendor].
- Provides a fixing for hold-down anchors against wind or seismic loads [Sec: OEP]. Bushman requires a ring beam for tanks in cyclonic regions [Prod].
- Keeps the liner on a granular bed, so it is less sensitive to the cracking that affects slabs.

**Disadvantages**

- More cost and time than a pad: excavation, steel, formwork, concrete and curing; a builder or civils contractor is usually needed [Prod: Coerco; Tank Factory].
- The ring must be accurately level and round; an out-of-level ring is built into the tank. Bolted-tank specifications ask for about ±3 mm in any 9 m of circumference and ±6 mm overall from a plane [Prod: Tank Connection, converted from 1/8 in in 30 ft and 1/4 in].
- The step between ring and fill must be detailed so the liner does not span a gap or bear on a hard corner.
- Concrete can crack and its reinforcement can corrode, especially in coastal air or with chlorides [Sec: OEP].
- The joint between wall and ring can trap water if a sealant debonds, and that drives pitting at the bottom of the wall [Sec: OEP]. Aluzinc needs a separator (section 9).
- Does not fix a weak subgrade under the middle of the tank; the fill can still settle in a dish.

**Typical published details**

- Minimum ringwall width about 0.3 m, and the tank bottom no more than about 0.3 m above outside grade [Sec: Padros, attributing both to API 650]. OEP gives 300–450 mm wide and 0.9–1.5 m deep below frost, unsourced, for large oil tanks; frost depth is not a design driver on most South African sites.
- Fill inside the ring of compacted sand or gravel [Sec: OEP; Tarsco]. GEI Works puts about 100 mm (4 in) of compacted sand inside the ring for corrugated liner tanks, and asks for a level area 3–4 m larger than the ring [Prod].
- Earth pressure from the fill on the ring is active on the inside; designing for at-rest pressure roughly doubled the hoop steel in Padros's example, so the ring's reinforcement needs real design, not a rule of thumb [Sec: Padros].
- PIP STE03020 sets minimum ringwall dimensions (Section 5) but the values were not in the text read [Sec: Bentley help].
- **No source gives ring beam dimensions or reinforcement for small corrugated liner tanks.** The oil-tank rules describe heavier shells than Damtech's.

**Where it fits.** Tanks from about 6 m diameter upward; any roofed tank in an exposed, windy position; sloping sites where the downhill edge would otherwise be built up in fill; erodible sandy or coastal sites; and any client who wants the tank to stay level and maintenance-free for its life.

## 5. Option C: full reinforced concrete slab

**What it is.** A reinforced concrete slab across the whole footprint, usually extending beyond the wall, with the tank wall standing on it and the liner on an underlay over the slab.

**How it complements the tank.** It makes the base a single, stiff, level surface that will not erode or rut, and it lets the owner see and control leaks: water that passes the liner runs off the slab edge or into a detection point instead of soaking into the subsoil. It also gives anchorage anywhere on the perimeter.

**Advantages**

- Uniform, level, durable surface; no erosion or edge washout [Prod: Tarsco; Coerco says concrete is "most long-lasting"].
- Maximum bearing capacity and a slab can span small soft spots when designed to [Sec: OEP; Tarsco].
- Leak control: a slab with a slight fall to the edge, or a perimeter detection point, makes liner leaks visible early [Inference; AWWA D100 asks for a slope up to the centre under steel floors, reported by ACI].
- Anchors can be cast or drilled anywhere.
- Preferred by fire-tank writers. One NFPA 22 article recommends a reinforced slab because sand or gravel bases "can risk foundational shifts" that compromise the liner [Sec: IFF, vendor-linked author].
- The natural choice on problem ground (section 8), where it becomes an engineered raft.

**Disadvantages**

- Highest cost, longest build, and 5–7 days or more of curing before loading [Prod: Tank Factory].
- A broom-finished or rough slab will abrade a liner; it needs a smooth finish plus a geotextile or underlay, which Damtech's bidem floor sheet partly provides [Inference; IFF recommends a geotextile pre-liner].
- A slab on poor ground still settles at the edge unless it is designed as a stiffened raft; a flat slab "does not inherently resist edge settlement" [Prod: Tarsco].
- Cracks from shrinkage, poor drainage or weak soil [Prod: Tarsco]. Thin slabs of 75–100 mm with mesh, common in poly-tank guides, are not adequate for a large water tank [Prod: Coerco; Tank Factory, poly context].
- Water trapped between liner and slab cannot drain into the ground, so a leak must have a planned path out.
- Aluzinc walls must be isolated from it (section 9); a galvanised wall should not be set on fresh concrete.
- Not relocatable.

**Typical published details.** Poly-tank guides give 75–100 mm slabs with mesh, extended about 300 mm beyond the tank [Prod: Coerco; Tank Factory]. These are not structural guidance for a 30 kPa, multi-tonne liner tank and should not be repeated as such. AWWA D100, as quoted by ACI, asks for a sand cushion of at least 25 mm, or a 12 mm fibre joint filler (ASTM D1751), between a steel bottom and a concrete slab [Sec: ACI FAQ]. Tank Connection uses the same bituminous filler between the floor and the ringwall [Prod].

**Where it fits.** Dolomite land (once the designation allows it), expansive or compressible ground, potable and fire-water tanks, tanks next to buildings or slopes where a leak would do damage, and owners who want the longest, lowest-maintenance base and will pay for it.

## 6. Option D: engineered foundations for problem ground

Where ground investigation shows significant heave, collapse, compressibility or dolomite hazard, the choice moves beyond the three standard options:

- **Stiffened or cellular raft.** The NHBRC approach for light structures on expansive clay where predicted heave exceeds about 15 mm; a Free State study recommends raft beams 600–900 mm deep, deeper than the standard NHBRC detail [Sec: Bester et al, ARCSMGE 2024]. The same family is listed for H2, H3, C2 and S2 site classes [Sec: Van Rooy, IAEG 2006, citing NHBRC/GFSH-2].
- **Soil raft or soil replacement** by removing and recompacting the problem layer [Sec: Van Rooy].
- **Compaction of in-situ soils** below the base on compressible or collapsible sands (C1, C2, S1 classes) [Sec: Van Rooy].
- **Piles or pile-supported ringwalls** for very soft ground, high water tables or uplift [Prod: Tarsco]. These are rarely economic for farm tanks.
- **Dolomite precautionary measures** to SANS 1936 (section 8), which centre on stopping concentrated water ingress.

These are engineered solutions. They need a geotechnical investigation and a designer, and they are outside what a supply-and-install price can include.

## 8. South African ground conditions

### Dolomite land (Gauteng, especially Centurion and parts of Tshwane)

- About 25% of Gauteng is dolomitic; sinkholes and subsidence are concentrated on the far West Rand, south of Pretoria and on the East Rand, and Centurion is a named sinkhole area [Sec: Council for Geoscience via Pretoria News, 2022; SRK, 2025].
- Leaking water-bearing services are the main trigger, quoted at "almost 95% of the time" by a University of Pretoria professor, along with poor stormwater control and groundwater drawdown [Sec: Pretoria News 2022; Kirsten et al, JSAICE 2014].
- SANS 1936 requires a dolomite stability investigation before development, and it classifies land as D1 to D4. From D2 upward, precautionary measures must prevent concentrated ingress of water into the ground [Sec: Kirsten et al, describing SANS 1936-1:2012]. New wet services must be designed to minimise leaks [Sec: Merafong MSDF 2019, citing SANS 1936-3].
- **No source read addresses water tanks directly.** The inference is direct, though: a tank is a concentrated water source, and a liner leak into a sand pad is exactly the concentrated ingress SANS 1936 seeks to prevent. On dolomite land, Damtech should ask for the site's dolomite designation before recommending a base, and favour a slab with a sealed, drained leak path and stormwater led away, designed with a competent person's input [Inference].

### Expansive (heaving) clays (Gauteng, Free State and elsewhere)

- Karoo Supergroup and Beaufort Group clays are expansive and cover large parts of Gauteng and the Free State, with parts of the Western and Eastern Cape [Sec: Bester et al, 2024]. Pretoria roadbed heave data and cases near Cape Town and Kimberley are reported [Sec: Netterberg, 2025].
- A local water source causes severe differential heave. In one Namibian case, predicted heave was 75 mm, but the damage came from "broken drains or leaking taps" [Sec: Netterberg, citing Donaldson 1973].
- NHBRC/GFSH-2 classes H to H3 escalate from normal construction to stiffened rafts, soil rafts or piles [Sec: Van Rooy]. An apron sloping away from the foundation is recommended to stop moisture migrating underneath [Sec: Bester et al].
- For a tank this means: a sand pad on heaving clay will move unevenly when it rains, when the tank overflows, or when the liner leaks. Use soil replacement or a stiffened slab, with a sloping apron and overflow piped well clear [Inference].

### Compressible and collapsible soils

- NHBRC classes C (potentially collapsible sands and silty sands) and S (compressible soils) call for compaction below foundations, stiffened rafts or soil rafts as severity rises [Sec: Van Rooy]. Collapse settlement of under 5 mm is class C and 5–10 mm is C1.
- The sources read do not locate collapsible soils by region, so this report does not claim specific areas. Wetting is the classic collapse trigger, which again makes leak control and drainage the key design point [Inference, general geotechnical knowledge].

### Coastal and wind-exposed sites (e.g. the Overstrand)

- Sandy coastal soils erode easily under run-off and wind; a confined pad, ring beam or slab is preferable to an open pad [Inference from the erosion findings in sections 3 and 4].
- Coastal air (C4–C5) shortens the life of both the steel at the wall base and the reinforcement in any concrete; the coating report covers the steel side ([research/aluzinc-vs-z250-water-tank-coatings.md](aluzinc-vs-z250-water-tank-coatings.md)).
- Strong winds make anchorage of roofed tanks more important, which favours a ring beam or slab.

### What to ask for before choosing

A site photo set, the slope, what the ground is (rock, sand, red soil, black clay, fill), whether it is on known dolomite land (municipal dolomite maps or a stability report), the tank size and whether it will have a roof, and how close it is to buildings, boundaries or slopes. Where any hazard shows up, recommend a geotechnical investigation before quoting the base.

## 9. Where steel meets the base: coatings, concrete and soil

This section connects to the coating report already in this folder.

- **Galvanised (Z275) on concrete.** Fresh concrete is about pH 12.5 or higher; zinc reacts with it and gives off hydrogen while the concrete cures, and mixes above pH 13.3 increase the reaction. Once the concrete has set, a calcium hydroxy-zincate layer passivates the zinc and attack stops [Sec: American Galvanizers Association]. Galvanising embedded in sound concrete is reported to corrode below 0.1 µm/year [Sec: GalvInfo, via the coating report]. So a galvanised wall can bear on cured concrete, but should not be set into wet concrete, and water that has stood on fresh concrete should be flushed before it touches the shell [Inference consistent with the coating report].
- **Aluzinc (AZ150) on concrete.** GalvInfo advises no contact between 55% Al-Zn and concrete, including cured concrete that may get wet, because alkalinity attacks the aluminium [Sec: GalvInfo, via the coating report]. A separator is required under the wall on any ring beam or slab.
- **The separator.** Bolted-tank practice puts a bituminous filler strip (ASTM D1751 type) between the steel and the concrete [Prod: Tank Connection; Sec: AWWA D100 via ACI]. A bitumen-impregnated fibre strip or a bituminous damp-proof course strip under the bottom angle, with a flexible sealed joint on the outside, is the practical South African equivalent [Inference; product choice to be confirmed by Damtech].
- **The sealed joint must stay bonded and drain.** A debonded sealant becomes a capillary trap that drives pitting at the bottom of the wall [Sec: OEP]. Detail the joint so water sheds outward, and inspect it.
- **Soil, gravel and vegetation off the wall.** Material against the bottom of the wall holds moisture and causes crevice corrosion [Sec: OEP]. Bolted-tank specifications forbid backfill against the sidewall without the manufacturer's approval [Prod: Tank Connection]. This applies to every base type and matches Damtech's existing vegetation guidance (blog: reservoir vegetation management).
- **Height above grade.** API 650 guidance keeps the tank bottom no more than about 0.3 m above outside grade for its own reasons [Sec: Padros]; OEP gives at least 150–300 mm above surrounding grade so the bottom stays dry [Sec, unsourced]. For corrugated tanks, the practical aim is a base top at least a little above finished ground, with ground falling away, so the wall base never sits in water [Inference].

## 10. What the published numbers say, and how far they carry

- **Most numbers are vendor figures and they conflict.** Pad depth runs from 70 mm to 150 mm and overhang from 150 mm to more than 1 m a side across Coerco, Tank Factory, Bushman and Pioneer. These reflect different tank types and different liability positions, not an agreed standard.
- **The standards read were second-hand.** API 650, API 653, PIP STE03020, AWWA D100/D103 and NFPA 22 were seen only through study guides, papers, specifications and articles. No clause text was read. Treat every attributed figure as "reported as", not as the standard's wording.
- **Oil-tank rules overstate the wall load.** API ringwall minimums (0.3 m wide) suit heavy welded shells. A corrugated liner tank's wall load is lighter, so a smaller ring may be adequate, but no source gives a sized ring for these tanks.
- **Levelness.** The only numeric tolerances found are bolted-tank figures: about ±3 mm in any 9 m of circumference and ±6 mm from a plane for a ringwall top, and ±1.5 mm per 3 m for a starter ring [Prod: Tank Connection]. They are a useful benchmark for a ring beam, not a requirement.
- **Wind uplift.** No source gave uplift loads or anchor requirements for empty corrugated tanks. SANS 10160-3 (wind actions) would govern a design check in South Africa; it was not read.
- **Costs.** No sourced South African rand rates were found; costs are ranked only.

## 11. Recommendation by tank and site

| Situation | Recommended base | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 3–6 m diameter kit, firm level ground, open position | Compacted crusher-dust or sand pad on geotextile, confined edge preferred | Damtech's current default. Add an erosion band of aggregate round the wall and a fall away from the pad. |
| 3–6 m kit with a roof in an exposed, windy position | Ring beam with compacted fill, or a slab | Gives anchorage; anchor detail by Damtech. |
| About 6 m diameter and above | Ring beam with compacted fill | Matches vendor practice from about 6 m (20 ft). Keeps the soft liner bed and a rigid, level wall seat. |
| Sloping site | Ring beam (with cut-and-fill platform) | Avoid an open granular pad built up on the downhill side. |
| Sandy or coastal, erodible ground | Ring beam, or confined pad for small tanks | Protect against wind and run-off scour; isolate Aluzinc. |
| Expansive clay | Soil replacement plus slab, or a stiffened raft; apron and overflow led away | Geotechnical advice for anything beyond small tanks. |

| Any concrete base with a galvanised shell | Erect only on cured concrete; separator strip recommended | Flush water that stood on fresh concrete. |

**In one line:** pad for small tanks on good ground, ring beam as the standard upgrade for larger, roofed, sloping or sandy sites, slab or engineered raft where the ground or the duty demands that leaks never reach the soil.

## 12. What the Pioneer catalogue does and does not give

The public Pioneer Water Tanks catalogues found online (a rural catalogue and an Acer/Pioneer fire-tank flyer, both hosted by a US dealer) say only that tanks can go on "a sand pad or a concrete ring beam", depending on the site and the applicable standard. The flyer's drawing shows a sand pad of about 150 mm minimum with a rock erosion layer and a level grade assumed. Neither document gives ring beam width, depth, reinforcement, concrete grade or a levelness tolerance, and the rural catalogue states that Pioneer is not a designer of water management systems. There is nothing in them for Damtech to adapt, and their designs or text should not be copied in any case. If Damtech holds a different Pioneer document with ring beam drawings, it should be read against this report, and any detail Damtech publishes should still be its own.
